Topic by topicThe seven topics and what gets asked

Same 80/20 split you saw at midterm: most of these are traceable procedures, not essays. That plays to what you already trained.

Local Search Week 8 · shared CO1-CO4
"Local Search Algorithms: Hill-climbing Search, Simulated Annealing, Local Beam Search."

The bridge topic — still search, but you keep only the current state and move to a better neighbour instead of building a tree.

  • Hill climbing: move to the best-scoring neighbour; stop when none is better
  • Its failure modes: local maximum/minimum, plateau, ridge
  • Simulated annealing: sometimes accept a worse move, with probability falling as "temperature" drops — escapes local optima
  • Local beam search: keep k states at once instead of one
You have a head start: the "Hill-climbing in Local Minima" slide in your 77-page note is this exact topic. It was final-term material sitting in your midterm note all along.
Simulation-style: run hill climbing on an 8-puzzle or a small state space, show h at each step, and identify where it gets stuck.
Genetic Algorithms Weeks 8 + 9 · double CO1-CO4
Wk 8: "Genetic Algorithm: Biological Background, Basic Outline, Encoding system, Crossover, Mutation, Selection" Wk 9: "Genetic Algorithm: Solving Example Problems using GA"

Your single biggest topic. Two weeks, and an entire week devoted to worked problems — which is the course telling you a numerical GA question is coming.

  • Population, chromosome, gene, encoding (binary, permutation, value)
  • Fitness function and fitness evaluation
  • Selection: roulette wheel, tournament, rank
  • Crossover: single-point, two-point, uniform
  • Mutation: bit flip, mutation rate
  • The full generational cycle; elitism; termination
Simulation-style — expect this as a 20-mark question: "Given this initial population and fitness function, perform two generations. Show fitness values, selection probabilities, the crossover at point k, the mutation, and the new population." It is a step-by-step trace exactly like UCS — same marks, same discipline of showing every intermediate table.
The two problems AIUB actually uses. Both recur in the department's own GA materials, so practise these exact shapes: (1) maximise f(x) = x²/2 − 3x for x ∈ [0,31] with 5-bit binary encoding; (2) 8-queens with fitness = number of non-attacking pairs, maximum 28 = n(n−1)/2. A real final-term GA 8-queens question with the instructor's own solution exists in a student lab repo.
Constraint Satisfaction Problems Weeks 10 + 11 · double CO3, CO4
Wk 10: "Constraint Satisfaction Problems: Define CSPs, Backtracking search for CSPs" Wk 11: "Improving backtracking efficiency: Most constrained variable- minimum remaining values (MRV), Variable and value ordering, Forward checking, constraints propagation-Arc consistency"

The other double-week topic, and the "CSP" named in CO3 that your midterm never touched.

  • Formulating a problem as a CSP: variables, domains, constraints; constraint graph
  • Standard examples: map colouring, N-queens, cryptarithmetic, Sudoku
  • Backtracking search — the base algorithm
  • MRV (most-constrained variable) and degree heuristic; least-constraining-value ordering
  • Forward checking — prune neighbours' domains on each assignment
  • Arc consistency / AC-3 — constraint propagation
Simulation-style — the classic: "Colour this map with 3 colours using backtracking with forward checking and MRV. Show the domain of every variable after each assignment, and where backtracking occurs." Domain tables are the equivalent of the frontier table — that is where the marks live.
Know the Australia map by heart. AIUB's own 49-slide CSP deck traces arc consistency on the Australia map-colouring problem across roughly 18 slides, ending with South Australia's domain wiped out to { }. That trace is the single most likely CSP exam question.
Statistical Reasoning — Probability and Bayes Week 12 CO3, CO4
"Statistical Reasoning: Probability, Bayes Theorem, Bayesian Network, Application of Bayes Theorem, Bayes network inference problem"

The "uncertainty" of CO3. Note the outline explicitly names application and inference problem — numerical, not descriptive.

  • Probability basics: joint, marginal, conditional probability, product rule
  • Bayes' theorem: P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / P(B)
  • Classic application: disease-test problems (true positive vs base rate)
  • Bayesian networks: nodes, directed edges, conditional probability tables
  • Reading independence off the graph; computing a joint probability by multiplying CPT entries
Simulation-style: "Given this Bayesian network and its CPTs, compute P(Alarm | Burglary), or the probability of a full assignment." Pure arithmetic with an unambiguous answer — the easiest marks on the paper once you have done five of them.
AIUB's own Bayes exercise set (from the department's Statistical Reasoning deck): meningitis and stiff neck; two bags of green and blue balls; 90% flu vs 10% measles with rash probabilities 0.08 and 0.95; three job applicants in ratio 1:2:4; a clinical-trial calculation. Work these five and you have seen every variation they ask.
Hidden Markov Models Lecturer's list only AIMA 15.1-15.3
"Lecture 11: Chapter 15 — Probabilistic Reasoning over Time: 15.1 Time and Uncertainty, 15.2 Inference in Temporal Models, 15.3 Hidden Markov Models"

On your moderator's lecture list but absent from the semester week-plan. It follows naturally from Bayesian networks, so it may be taught briefly or skipped entirely.

  • Why time changes probabilistic reasoning; the Markov assumption
  • Transition model and sensor (observation) model
  • Filtering, prediction, smoothing, most-likely explanation
Treat as low priority until confirmed — most likely a conceptual question if it appears at all. Ask your teacher directly whether HMMs are examinable.
Learning from Examples Lecturer's list only AIMA 18.1-18.7
"Lecture 12: Chapter 18 — Learning from Examples: 18.1 Forms of Learning, 18.2 Supervised Learning, 18.6 Regression and Classification with Linear Models, 18.7 Artificial Neural Networks"

The lecturer's list wraps neural networks inside a wider machine-learning lecture. The extra material beyond ANN:

  • Forms of learning: supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement
  • Supervised learning setup: training set, hypothesis, generalisation, overfitting
  • Linear regression and linear classification; the decision boundary
Cheap insurance: the definitions here take fifteen minutes and cover you if the paper opens the ANN question with "define supervised learning."
Artificial Neural Networks Week 13 CO3, CO4
"Introduction to Artificial Neural Networks: Objectives, History, Applications and Biological Inspiration of Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), ANN Architecture. Backpropagation Algorithm, Learning using Backpropagation"

Half descriptive (history, inspiration, applications), half arithmetic (architecture, backprop). AIUB's official catalog also lists "solve problems using perceptron learning rules" as a course objective, so expect perceptron arithmetic too.

  • Biological neuron → artificial neuron; why ANNs
  • Weights, bias, weighted sum, activation functions (step, sigmoid, ReLU, tanh)
  • ANN architecture: input / hidden / output layers, feed-forward computation
  • Perceptron learning rule: w ← w + η(target − output)·x; AND/OR solvable, XOR not
  • Backpropagation: forward pass, error at output, propagate backward, update weights
Simulation-style: "Given these inputs, weights and bias, compute the network output", and "perform one iteration of the perceptron learning rule / one backpropagation weight update showing each calculation."
Theory half: draw and label an ANN architecture; explain biological inspiration; list applications.
Expert Systems Week 14 CO3, CO4
"Expert System: Introduction, Architecture, Participants, and Components of Expert System. Review, Discussion, Open problems, and Brainstorming"

The course's namesake topic, taught last, and almost entirely descriptive. Note that week 14 doubles as revision — so this is a light week, likely a smaller question.

  • What an expert system is; why and when to use one
  • Architecture: knowledge base, inference engine, working memory, explanation facility, user interface, knowledge-acquisition module
  • Participants: domain expert, knowledge engineer, end user
  • Forward vs backward chaining in the inference engine
  • Advantages, limitations, classic systems (MYCIN, DENDRAL)
Likely question if it appears: "Draw the architecture of an expert system and describe each component / the participants." The direct analogue of the midterm's "draw a utility-based agent" — pure memorisation, twenty minutes of work.
One caution: Expert Systems sits in the last teaching week, in a row that also reads "Review, Discussion, Open problems, and Brainstorming" — the week most often eaten by revision. It also does not appear on your moderator's own lecture list. Learn it (it is cheap), but do not build your revision around it.

Where to startThe order that pays

  1. Genetic algorithms — two weeks, one of them pure worked problems. Highest expected marks, and it is a mechanical trace like UCS.
  2. CSP with forward checking and MRV — the other double week. Domain tables are your new frontier tables.
  3. Bayes theorem — small, self-contained, unambiguous arithmetic.
  4. Perceptron and backprop arithmetic — practise two or three by hand.
  5. Expert system architecture and local search definitions — memorisation; save for last.
